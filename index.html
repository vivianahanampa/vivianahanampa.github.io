About

the short version

I'm a data scientist and researcher finishing an M.S. in Data Science at Columbia University, working on how AI models and the brain represent the world. In the Mesgarani Lab I build fMRI encoding models that predict brain activity from vision and language model features. In the Anacker Lab at Columbia Psychiatry I'm building an AI video pipeline that tracks interacting mice, to test how one brain circuit shapes coping with social stress. And I first-authored PocketSign, a sign language recognition model small enough to run in a web browser.

Before Columbia I spent three years shipping machine learning in Lima, Peru. At Interbank I built credit-risk models serving 50,000+ customers and a retrieval-augmented generation system over financial filings. At Telefónica I deployed clustering models that cut field response times by 85%. At IBM I built a real-time fraud-detection pipeline adopted by seven enterprise clients.

I trained as an electronic engineer first, which is probably why I like problems that run all the way from raw signal to deployed system. Research interests: brain–computer interfaces, machine learning for neural and physiological signals, AI models and the brain, and AI for health.

Projects

questions, not demos

PocketSign

Under review · VISAPP 2027

Can a sign language model small enough to run in a browser match one pretrained on 1,985 hours of video?

A compact model that recognizes signs from body-movement keypoints, built for sign languages with very little data, such as Peruvian and Argentinian Sign Language. It uses no pretraining, and an interpretable body-part selection that adds up to 9.3 points and matches linguists' one- versus two-handed labels (AUC 0.89). First author, with Prof. Gissella Bejarano's group at Marist University.

ResultIt matches the state-of-the-art pretrained model while being 956 times smaller: 1.25 points ahead on Argentinian and on par on Peruvian Sign Language. At 2.46 MB it runs in milliseconds on a laptop or in a browser, 56 times faster on CPU.

Vision-language models and the human brain

In progress

Does training a model with language make it more brain-like — or does vision alone get there first?

Encoding models that predict the fMRI activity of 4 people watching Friends across 1,000 cortical regions, from vision and language model representations (DINOv3, SigLIP2, Qwen3). Trained on seasons 1–5 and tested on an unseen season 6. NeuroTech Columbia, Mesgarani Lab.

ResultA self-supervised vision model predicts the brain 31% better than a language-supervised one, in all 4 participants. Combining vision and language features adds another 18% (mean r = 0.18, non-overlapping 95% CIs).

EEG-to-text decoding

Prototype

Can an 8-channel EEG headset recover the words someone is reading or hearing — and can the brain's own surprise signal catch the decoder's mistakes?

Contrastive learning maps 8-channel EEG into T5 word-embedding space, then retrieves candidate words for each position. N400 features — the brain's response to a semantically unexpected word — flag implausible candidates, and a language model reconstructs the sentence from what's left. Built with Hazel Lim, on the open codebase from Decoding individual words from non-invasive brain recordings.

Deep RL for competitive Pokémon

Complete

Can an agent learn competitive play well enough to consistently beat scripted opponents?

A Rainbow-style DQN — dueling heads, noisy-net exploration, prioritized replay with 3-step returns — trained over 2,000 Gen 1 battles on a local Pokémon Showdown server through UT Austin's metamon environment. 150+ engineered state features with reward shaping for long-horizon planning, and a 100K-transition replay buffer that cut training time 60%.

ResultIt wins up to 70% of evaluation battles against a random opponent, but no more than 15% against the scripted Gen 1 boss AI. Consistently beating scripted play is still open.
